          What this does that a typing test does not

          • It works on what you need, inside normal text. Every key, every letter pair and every word start is timed. The slowest and most error-prone become targets, placed inside ordinary words, a different carrier word each time, several times per test at growing gaps. You never see which words are carriers. Interleaving weak items into mixed practice beats drilling them in a block for retention and transfer (Shea and Morgan 1979; Schorn and Knowlton 2021 for finger sequences). Spaced, varied repetitions build word-level chunks (Yamaguchi and Logan 2014, skilled typists).
          • Your score comes from text the coach did not touch. Real-text tests use unaltered quotes from the Monkeytype pool, so the number is comparable and cannot be inflated by adapted text. The first ten set your baseline; the review date is four weeks later.
          • Speed is built clean, a little at a time. In the largest study of typing (136 million keystrokes, Dhakal and colleagues 2018) the fastest typists were also the most accurate, and their speed came from an even rhythm and overlapping keys, not from forcing. Practising errors rehearses them. So the bursts count only correct letters, the target sits just above your recent clean speed, and a burst under the accuracy floor brings the target down at once. Bursts come in pairs: a steady one at 90% of the target to groove a clean rhythm on the common words you type slowest (skilled typists type familiar words as single units, Yamaguchi and Logan 2014), then a stretch at the target, and one accuracy test straight after the block: speed and control train best in alternation, building speed and then keeping only what you can control (West). In everyday typists the one habit that predicted speed was meaning to type fast in real typing (Keith and Ericsson 2007).
          • It trains rolls. Fast typists overlap keys, the next key going down before the last comes up, on 40 to 70% of keystrokes (Dhakal 2018). The models of skilled typing explain why: a familiar word is prepared as one unit and its keystrokes are launched in parallel, so overlap is what a well-learned word looks like (Rumelhart and Norman 1982; Logan and Crump 2011). Overlap also happens whenever the gap between keys is shorter than how long a key is held, so holding keys longer would raise it without making you faster. So the roll test measures the time between the two keys of each pair first and overlap second, and it builds the units directly: common words carrying your slowest frequent pairs, each typed three times a few words apart and faster each time, with mistakes counted but never stopping you. The same words are mixed into the steady speed bursts. Keys your keyboard holds back until release (home-row mods) cannot overlap, so their pairs are left out. Nobody has trialled roll training itself; the numbers after each test show whether it is working for you.
          • Bursts use the whole vocabulary, not a short-word list. Gains on short-common-word "speed sentences" (Monkeytype's english 200 is one) were found spurious: they transfer only to text made of the same sequences, and transfer to all text needs practice on all text (West). Faster typists are tuned to the bigram and trigram statistics of ordinary English (Behmer and Crump 2017), which only ordinary text carries. Your own sentences, lowercased, are used half the time when you have pasted them in.
          • Confusions get discrimination practice, not repetition. The one accuracy tactic the trials supported was jamming commonly confused letters together so the fingers learn the difference (West). When you keep typing one letter for another, words that hold both letters close together are woven into your text until the confusion stops showing. Repeating the mistyped words themselves was found useless, so the word schedule tracks slowness, not errors.
          • It stays quiet unless you leave the band. One number and, only when needed, one sentence. Feedback after every trial speeds acquisition but weakens retention (Schmidt 1989; Winstein and Schmidt 1990). Nothing is highlighted while you type, because attention to the hands disrupts skilled typing (Logan and Crump 2009). The interface fades while you type for the same reason.
          • It makes the common words automatic. Every common word's burst speed is tracked across sessions. The slow ones, weighted by how common they are, are placed in your text as themselves and climb a four-box spaced schedule (1, 2, 4, 8 days) until they are near your median. The 300 most common words are about two thirds of everything you type, and skilled speed is word-level chunks (Logan and Crump 2011).
          • It plans across days. Yesterday's accuracy and burst pass rate decide whether today is a speed day or an accuracy day, the way the typewriting trials alternated speed-forcing and control days (West). A session that fades gets shortened.
          • It measures your eye-hand span. A one-minute check with 0 to 3 words of preview, after Salthouse's preview-window method, shows whether you actually read ahead and whether the read-ahead option is worth your time.
          • It keeps a ledger of what slows you down. At the end of every session twelve measures are checked against norms from the typing literature: accuracy, correction load (keys per character) and backspaces per error, speed lost to errors, hesitation at word starts and before symbols, the cost of capitals, early letters and transpositions, whether errors follow a rhythm wobble (Kalfaoglu and Stafford 2014), how fast you recover after a fix (Crump and Logan 2013), keystroke overlap, and dropped mod letters. Each flagged issue changes the next session: read-ahead switched on, a pace line to hold you back, extra Shift or symbol tests, word-delete corrections. The ledger shows when each issue was first seen and whether it is fading.
          • It trains the moves, not just the letters. Most of a day at a keyboard is editing and moving, not typing fresh text. The editing block gives you a task in a real text field, times the move, and repeats the slow ones. The symbols block uses the characters of chat, notes and email rather than code unless you turn code on.
          • Accuracy counts every wrong key, fixed or not. That is Monkeytype's definition and it is the right one: across 168,000 typists, the share of keypresses spent correcting predicted speed better than the errors left in the text (r = 0.36 against 0.21), and the fastest tenth press 1.10 keys per character where the slowest press 1.24 (Dhakal, Feit, Kristensson and Oulasvirta 2018). A fixed error still costs the wrong key, the backspaces, the retype and a broken rhythm. The aim is a first pass that needs no fixing; the keys-per-character number on every result shows the whole cost.
          • It can train on your own words. Paste a page of what you really type into Settings and most accuracy tests are built from your own sentences; your own frequent words are tracked and woven in. Skilled speed is word-level chunks (Logan and Crump 2011), and the chunks that pay are the words you use.
          • It measures composing, not only copying. A compose block in every session: ninety seconds of writing to a prompt with no target text. It separates thinking time from typing time and counts revisions, so you can see whether the keyboard is the bottleneck at all. The text is never stored.
          • It checks whether you look at the keyboard. A covered-hands check: one 30-second test uncovered, one with a towel over the hands. Gaze shifts to the keyboard track the gap between keys (Feit, Weir and Oulasvirta 2016). If covering costs you speed, the warm-up asks for covered hands for two weeks.
          • It proves each change on you. Experiments in Settings switch one thing on and off across twelve alternating sessions and score only the unaltered real-text tests. What moved the score stays; what did not goes.
          • It knows your board. Symbol routes are read from your Miryoku layout, so a slow symbol is named by its layer, key and finger.
          • It names your errors. Each miss is classified: the next letter typed early (anticipation, a rhythm problem), a neighbouring key (a reach problem), the previous letter repeated, or something else. The hesitation map shows where your pauses live: before capitals, before punctuation, at word starts, or inside words.
          • It coaches the home-row Shift. Every capital is checked. The mod's own letter appearing means the hold was read as a tap; lowercase means Shift was released early; a capitalised next letter means it was held too long; Shift released before the letter came up means the capital only worked because you waited out the tapping term. It also says which hand's Shift the failed capital needed.
          • Numbers match Monkeytype. Net wpm counts only fully correct words plus their space. Raw counts everything typed. Accuracy is correct keypresses over all keypresses. Consistency is Monkeytype's kogasa function over per-second raw speed.
          • It stops you. One full session is about 14 minutes of typing, 18 to 20 with the pauses. Practice spread out beats practice crammed together: one hour a day beat two-hour blocks (Baddeley and Longman 1978). If you want more, add the short session (about 10 minutes) later in the day rather than making one session longer. and the overnight gain on finger sequences is about 20 percent without further practice (Walker and colleagues 2002).

          What it cannot do

          • It cannot see your real work yet. The editing block covers the standard macOS moves, but app shortcuts, window switching and anything the mouse still does are outside it.
          • The parts are evidence-based; nobody has run a trial of this exact combination. Judge it by the real-text score at the review date.
